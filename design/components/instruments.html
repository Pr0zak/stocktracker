StockTracker · Components

Instruments

Three small drawings that shared one defect: a reader could not tell what the mark was measured from. A sparkline scaled to its own range, a gauge knob on an unlabelled ramp, a benchmark line separated from the price only by hue. Each fix adds a baseline, a scale, or a dash — none of them adds a colour.

Sparkline · the baseline

was a real bug

The series is the intraday day, scaled to its own min and max, so it starts at the first print. The percentage beside it is measured from the previous close. A day that opens below yesterday and recovers therefore climbs left to right, in red, next to a negative number — observed live on BTC. One dashed line at the close reconciles them.

BTC $64,769 ▼ −0.10%

Before. The line goes up and the number is red. Both correct, and the row reads as broken.

BTC $64,769 ▼ −0.10%

After. Recovered all day, still under yesterday's close. The close joins the vertical scale rather than sitting outside it — otherwise a close beyond the day's range would be clamped to an edge by the very line meant to explain the number.

Benchmark overlay

reference, not verdict

Drawn in pink against a green price line, this was the worst pairing available for red-green colour vision deficiency — and it also claimed a semantic colour for something that is neither a gain nor a loss. Neutral and dashed fixes both at once. The legend swatch dashes to match; a key that shows solid for a dashed line is the one place a legend must not lie.

The same rule covers the cost-basis line and the 200-week line: a reference is grey and dashed, and never borrows gain or loss.

VIX gauge · the missing scale

inverted — high is the bad end

This one was less broken than it looked. The bands were already discrete and the zone pill already said “Calm” in words, so the reading never depended on hue alone. What was missing was a scale: a knob three-fifths along a five-colour ramp only says “20” if you can separate amber from orange. Boundary values, hairlines between bands, and a pointer make the position readable numerically.

Before — position on a ramp, no reference
After — bands countable, position legible

The shared lesson. All three were failures of reference, not of colour choice. A mark is only readable against something — yesterday's close, a boundary value, a line you can tell apart by its dashes. Adding hue would have fixed none of them, and in two cases hue was the thing making it worse. Open question: the sparkline baseline is drawn at 45% opacity, which survives on both themes but nearly disappears against a heavily-shaded area fill. If the row ever gains an area under the line, the baseline needs to move above it in z-order rather than gaining contrast.
