Design review · v0.80.0

What a good trading UI does, and where StockTracker doesn't

Five changes, ordered by how much they improve the app per hour of work. Every mockup below is built from the app's own tokens — GainGreen #2E9E57, LossRed #B0543D, amber #B0872B — so nothing here needs a new palette.

What the research actually says

Four findings that contradict the instinct to simplify.

  • Density is the featureFintech users are not casual browsers — they came for balances, risk, performance. The advice is “every pixel accountable”, not “fewer things”.
  • Hierarchy is earned by importanceNot by decoration, and not by what was built most recently. The thing the user opened the app to see goes first.
  • Short, interrupted, one-handedDesign for a glance at a bus stop, not a study session. The headline number must survive being seen for two seconds.
  • Red/green fails ~8% of menDeuteranopia. Colour must never be the only channel — pair it with an arrow, a sign, or a shape.

What the app already gets right

Worth stating, because three of these are things teams routinely get wrong and they're the reason the remaining fixes are small rather than structural.

  • Direction is double-encoded. Every change carries ▲/▼ and a sign alongside the colour. That is the single most-cited accessibility rule in financial UI, and it's already done.
  • Prices are monospaced with tabular figures, so columns of digits line up and scan.
  • The Portfolio header is textbook — value, today, total return, versus the S&P, and max drawdown, in that order, above a benchmarked chart.
  • Absent data says it is absent rather than rendering as zero or as calm.

The five changes

1 · Put the portfolio number on the Watchlist

hierarchy

The Watchlist opens with four stacked context cards — Dip radar, Market Session, Regime, VIX — before a single holding. The first ticker starts roughly 1,000 px down. Meanwhile the number most people open the app for, what am I worth and what did today do, is on a different tab.

Why. Four cards collapse into one status strip that says the same four things in one line. The headline number arrives first, and three holdings are visible where one was.

2 · Tighten the rows — same content, twice the list

density

Rows are ~150 px tall for one ticker. The content doesn't need that; the padding does. Robinhood and Webull fit six to eight names in the space this fits two and a half.

Why. Price and change move onto one line; the company name becomes optional (it's on the detail screen). Nothing is removed — the list just stops wasting vertical space on padding a scanning user never reads.

3 · The VIX gauge and the portfolio chart fail colourblind users

accessibility

The app is careful about this everywhere else — ▲/▼ on every change. Two places break the rule. The VIX gauge is a green→red rainbow with a dot on it and no number scale, so the position of the dot is the only signal. And the Portfolio chart draws the portfolio in green against a benchmark in pink — the single worst pairing for deuteranopia.

Why. The gauge gains discrete bands, a numeric scale and a pointer, so position is readable without hue. The benchmark becomes a dashed neutral — it isn't a gain or a loss, so it shouldn't be spending a semantic colour at all. Both survive greyscale.

4 · A rising sparkline next to a red number

real bug

On the live screen BTC shows a sparkline climbing left to right, coloured red, beside ▼ −213.83 (−0.10%). Both are correct and they contradict each other: MarketRepository.sparkline() plots ChartRange.DAY starting at the first intraday point, while the percentage is measured from the previous close. When the day opens below yesterday's close and recovers, the line rises and the number stays negative.

Why. One dashed line at yesterday's close makes the whole row consistent: the price recovered all day and still finished under the baseline, which is exactly what the red number says. Without it the row looks broken. Cheapest fix on this page.

5 · Holdings should beat the form

hierarchy

On Portfolio, a “Cash to invest” text field and two buttons sit between the chart and the holdings. A form outranks the data on a screen called Portfolio. Move the actions below the positions, or into the app bar.
